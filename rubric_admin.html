Section 01

What Is Secret Shopping?

Secret shopping is straightforward: an internal employee poses as a real stakeholder and walks through a journey end-to-end, without revealing who they are or asking for special treatment. If they get stuck, that is the finding.

The goal is not to find code bugs — that's what QA is for. We're auditing the emotional and functional integrity of the experience: does it feel trustworthy, clear, and appropriately effortful? Where does it break down, confuse, or disappoint?

Every finding gets tagged with one of four observation types:

🚧
Dead End
The user has no clear next step. The journey stops. Something is missing or unreachable.
🪟
Broken Window
A small defect — a typo, a misaligned element, a slow loader — that signals a lack of care and erodes trust.
🪚
Friction
A paper cut. The task is completable, but harder than it needs to be. Too many steps, too much effort.
✨
Missed Opportunity
Everything works, but something could be genuinely better — more personal, more helpful, more memorable.
Section 02

How Shoppers Score

Each dimension in your rubric gets a 1–5 numeric score plus a tagged observation type and written notes. There is no single rollup score per journey — we keep dimension scores separate so we can pinpoint exactly what's broken rather than averaging it away.

Notes are required for any score of 1, 2, 4, or 5. A score of 3 can stand alone.

ScoreMeaningNote requirement
1 — PoorBroken, missing, or actively harmful. Task may have failed.Required: what happened, what should have happened.
2 — Below expectationsWorks but with significant friction. Workarounds needed.Required: describe the friction and its impact.
3 — AcceptableFunctional but unremarkable. Table stakes.Optional.
4 — GoodAbove average; better than most would deliver.Required: what made it stand out.
5 — ExcellentBest-in-class. A reference example.Required: what specifically was excellent.

Employer / HR Buyer Journey

Covers the full arc from a prospect requesting a demo through an existing customer managing their account. Two sub-scenarios: Prospect and Existing Customer.

Prospect scenario

You are an HR Director at a 5,000-person company evaluating mental health benefits. You request a demo from the Spring Health website and go through the full sales process: first contact → discovery call → demo → proposal.

Existing customer scenario

You are an HR manager at an existing employer client. You log into the employer portal, pull a utilization report, and contact your CSM with a question about renewal terms.

Things to consider as you build your rubric:

  • ?What are the 3–5 moments in the sales process most likely to make or break a deal? Those are dimension candidates.
  • ?What does a truly well-run discovery call look like vs. a pitch-only call? How would a shopper know the difference?
  • ?What do employer admins try to do in the portal most often, and where do they currently get stuck?
  • ?What would a great ROI report look like vs. a technically-complete-but-useless one?
  • ?What's the handoff from sales to implementation like today — and what does "good" look like vs. what's the failure mode?
  • ?Are there compliance or data-security touchpoints in the employer journey that shoppers should specifically probe?

Provider Journey

Covers everything from a therapist's first contact with the credentialing process through day-to-day clinical work on the platform.

Scenario

You are a licensed therapist interested in joining the Spring Health network. You begin the credentialing application, go through onboarding training, and use the clinical platform to complete a simulated session workflow (where test access is available).

Things to consider as you build your rubric:

  • ?What is the credentialing process like today — where are the known friction points, and what would "fixed" look like?
  • ?What do providers most commonly complain about in clinical tools? Those pain points should have dimensions.
  • ?How should a shopper measure admin burden — what's a concrete proxy for "too much non-clinical work"?
  • ?What does great onboarding training look like vs. bare-minimum? How would a shopper distinguish them?
  • ?Is billing clarity a known issue? What would a shopper specifically look for to score it fairly?
  • ?Are there any parts of the provider journey that only surface after weeks of use? Flag those — we may need a longer-format shop for them.

Administrative / Ops Journey

A set of discrete sub-shops testing our ability to respond to operational requests: medical records, billing, crisis escalation, data reporting, and complaints.

Sub-shops (can be split across multiple shoppers)

Submit a medical records ROI request as a member. Raise a billing dispute as a member or employer admin. Test what happens when a member indicates they are in crisis. Submit a data/reporting request as an employer admin. File a formal complaint.

Things to consider as you build your rubric:

  • ?For each sub-shop, what is the SLA you'd want to hold yourself to? That becomes the benchmark for a score of 3 vs. 5.
  • ?What does the records request process look like today — is there a documented path, or is it ad hoc? How should a shopper find it?
  • ?What's the expected crisis escalation path? The shopper should be evaluating whether it's discoverable and functional without prior knowledge.
  • ?Are there compliance requirements (HIPAA, ERISA) that create hard pass/fail criteria for any of these sub-shops?
  • ?Who receives a complaint today, and what is the expected response process? If that isn't documented, the rubric itself will surface a gap.
  • ?Which of these sub-shops do you want to use real test accounts for vs. going fully blind? That affects scenario design.

Section 04

What Makes a Good Rubric Dimension?

Dimensions are the individual things shoppers score. A well-written dimension has three properties:

🎯
Observable without inside knowledge A shopper who knows nothing about your team should be able to evaluate it. If scoring it requires knowing your roadmap or internal architecture, rewrite it.
📏
Has a clear failure mode and a clear excellent state You should be able to describe, in a sentence each, what a 1 looks like and what a 5 looks like. If you can't, the dimension is too vague to score consistently.
🔬
Specific enough to route If a dimension scores a 1, it should be obvious which team owns the fix. "Overall experience" is too broad. "Time from demo request to first human contact" is specific enough.
📦
Scope: aim for 5–10 dimensions Fewer than 5 leaves too many gaps. More than 10 and shoppers lose attention. 6–8 is the sweet spot for most journeys. You can always add dimensions in a later quarter.
🚫
Don't test what you can already measure If you already have a metric for it (CSAT, time-to-credential, NPS), don't make it a rubric dimension — you have the data. Secret shopping is for things that don't show up in dashboards.
